  \begin{abstract}
  \noindent
  We present a novel method for normalizing the results of a mixed-gender
  indoor-rowing relay competition based on power as performance quantifier.
  The R{\aa}da Rowing Challenge 2026 involved ten five-person relay teams with
  different gender compositions, making direct comparison of raw distances
  problematic.
  We introduce a power-based normalization scheme in which each team's average
  power output is compared to a composition-weighted reference derived from
  world indoor-rowing records.
  We show that this approach is physically better motivated than a linear
  time-based normalization, because rowing performance scales as the cube of
  the rowing velocity.
  The method is applied to the competition results, and we discuss how the
  ranking differs from a naive distance ranking.
  \end{abstract}
  \vspace{1em}

\section{Introduction}

The R{\aa}da Rowing Challenge (RRC) is an indoor rowing competition organized
by M{\"o}lndals Roddklubb~\cite{MRKhomepage}, held annually at R{\aa}daRum
in M{\"o}lnlycke, Sweden.
The 2026 edition took place on 7~March 2026 and included individual events as
well as a 30-minute mixed-relay format~\cite{MRKweb2026}.
In the relay, each team consists of five rowers who take turns on a Concept2
RowErg ergometer; the combined distance covered by all five during the
30-minute window is the team's result.

Ten teams from clubs across the Gothenburg region entered the relay:
Chalmers Roddklubb (two teams), J{\"o}nk{\"o}pings Rodds{\"a}llskap (two teams),
Halmstads Roddklubb, M{\"o}lndals Roddklubb (two teams), G{\"o}teborgs
Roddklubb, R{\aa}da Crew, and CTH-GU.
Team compositions ranged from all-female (G{\"o}teborgs Roddklubb) to
four-men-one-woman (Chalmers Roddklubb -- Team~2), reflecting the mixed nature
of the competition.

Because men and women differ systematically in their power output on the
ergometer, a simple comparison of total relay distances would favour teams
with more male rowers.
To enable a fair ranking across different gender compositions, we develop a
normalization procedure described in the following sections.
Section~\ref{sec:formula} introduces the ergometer power formula.
Section~\ref{sec:records} summarizes relevant world records and the
performance gap between genders.
Section~\ref{sec:normalization} presents two normalization approaches and
argues for power-based normalization.
Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the competition results.

\section{The Ergometer Power Formula}
\label{sec:formula}

\subsection{Derivation}

The Concept2 ergometer flywheel decelerates between drive phases due to
air resistance.
The drag force scales quadratically with the flywheel angular velocity,
which is proportional to the handle velocity $v$ of the rower.
Since power equals force times velocity, the average power delivered to
the flywheel is~\cite{Tipler2008}
\begin{equation}
  P = c \, v^{3},
  \label{eq:power}
\end{equation}
where $v = d/T$ is the average rowing velocity (distance $d$ in metres,
time $T$ in seconds) and $c$ is the flywheel drag constant.
For the Concept2 RowErg~\cite{Concept2manual},
\begin{equation}
  c = \SI{2.8}{\watt\second\cubed\per\metre\cubed}.
  \label{eq:constant}
\end{equation}
This value is Concept2's published calibration constant, determined
empirically from flywheel drag measurements~\cite{Concept2manual}.
Physically, $c$ encodes the aerodynamic properties of the flywheel and
its housing; the monitor's internal algorithm normalises for damper-setting
variations, rendering $c$ effectively universal across machines.
The exact machine-to-machine variation of $c$ is not publicly documented;
however, any systematic offset in $c$ cancels identically in the
normalized score $S_k = P_k^{\mathrm{act}}/P_k^{\mathrm{ref}}$
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:score}), provided all teams row on comparable machines.
An independent validation study using force sensors found that the Concept2
monitor underestimates absolute power by approximately \SI{25}{\watt}
($\approx$7\%) at trained-rower intensities, with the offset being largely
independent of power level~\cite{Boyas2006}; because it appears in both
numerator and denominator of $S_k$, this systematic bias cancels in the
normalized score.
Equation~\eqref{eq:power} can equivalently be expressed using the
\emph{500-metre split time} $t_{500}$, the time to row \SI{500}{m}
at the current average pace:
\begin{equation}
  P = 2.8\left(\frac{500\,\mathrm{m}}{t_{500}}\right)^{\!3},
  \qquad
  t_{500} = \frac{500\,\mathrm{m} \cdot T}{d}.
  \label{eq:power_split}
\end{equation}
A faster split (smaller $t_{500}$) gives higher power, as physically expected.
For example, the men's reference pace (2000\,m in 334.7\,s) corresponds to
$t_{500} = 500 \times 334.7 / 2000 \approx 84$\,s (split 1:24\,min).

Figure~\ref{fig:power_curve} shows $P$ as a function of $t_{500}$
over the range typical for competitive indoor rowing.
The strong nonlinearity -- power grows as the \emph{cube} of
velocity -- is the central motivation for using power rather than
velocity or split time as the performance metric.

\subsection{Performance spread and the gender gap}

To characterise the performance spread, we define the top-athlete range
as world-record power down to \SI{80}{\percent} of that value.
For men this gives \SIrange{478}{597}{\watt} (2000\,m times 5:35--5:59);
for women the corresponding range is \SIrange{324}{405}{\watt}
(6:21--6:50).
The absolute power gap between the two reference levels in
Table~\ref{tab:records} is about \SI{47}{\percent}
(597\,W vs.\ 405\,W for 2000\,m).
In split time, the same gap is only \SI{14}{\percent}
(1:23.7 vs.\ 1:35.3 per 500\,m).
This asymmetry is a direct consequence of the cubic relation $P \propto v^3$:
a moderate advantage in speed translates into a much larger advantage in
power.
As a result, the gender composition of a relay team has a strong systematic
effect on the expected power output, motivating the normalization
approach described in the next section.

\subsection{Power as performance measure}
\label{sec:refdistance}

The normalization requires a choice of reference distance (and associated
world record).
In a 30-minute relay with five rowers, each member accumulates roughly
6 minutes of rowing, but not as a continuous effort: teams typically swap
every \SI{30}{\second} or so, meaning each rower completes approximately
12 sprint bursts of \SI{30}{\second}, covering a total distance of
well under \SI{2000}{m}.
The 2000\,m world record, corresponding to an approximately 6-minute
all-out effort, therefore matches both the total accumulated distance
per rower and the overall duration of each rower's contribution to the
relay most closely.
We therefore adopt the 2000\,m world record as the normalization
reference throughout (Section~\ref{sec:results}), and discuss the
sensitivity to this choice below.

To quantify the sensitivity to this choice, we compute the male/female
reference-power ratio at all four available distances
(Table~\ref{tab:records}):
\begin{align}
  \frac{P_m^{500}}{P_f^{500}}   &= \frac{1027}{580}  \approx 1.77, \\
  \frac{P_m^{1\text{k}}}{P_f^{1\text{k}}} &= \frac{710}{412}   \approx 1.72, \\
  \frac{P_m^{2\text{k}}}{P_f^{2\text{k}}} &= \frac{597}{405}   \approx 1.47, \\
  \frac{P_m^{5\text{k}}}{P_f^{5\text{k}}} &= \frac{490}{341}   \approx 1.44.
\end{align}
The male/female power gap decreases monotonically with distance, reflecting
the greater male advantage in short, anaerobic efforts relative to longer
aerobic exercise.
Choosing a shorter-distance reference therefore sets a more demanding
standard for male rowers (and conversely favours female-heavy teams),
while a longer-distance reference narrows the gap.
Notably, the 2000\,m and 5000\,m ratios (1.47 and 1.44) are very close
to each other -- a relative difference of only $\sim$2\% -- whereas the
step from 1000\,m to 2000\,m is considerably larger ($\sim$16\%).
This suggests that the normalization is relatively insensitive to the
choice between 2000\,m and 5000\,m references, which is reassuring given
the ambiguity in the appropriate reference distance for the relay format.

\section{Normalization}
\label{sec:normalization}

Let team $k$ comprise $n_m$ male and $n_f$ female rowers,
with $n = n_m + n_f$.
During the 30-minute relay ($T = 1800$\,s) the team covers a total
distance $D_k$, giving an average team power
\begin{equation}
  P_k^{\mathrm{act}} = 2.8\left(\frac{D_k}{T}\right)^{\!3}.
  \label{eq:team_power}
\end{equation}
To normalize this total distance we consider two approaches: a time-based
and a power-based normalization.

\subsection{Time-based normalization}

A natural first approach is to compare $D_k$ to the distance a reference
team of the same composition would cover at the gender-specific reference
pace.
Defining $v_m = d_{\mathrm{ref}}/T_m^{\mathrm{ref}}$ and
$v_f = d_{\mathrm{ref}}/T_f^{\mathrm{ref}}$ as the male and female
reference velocities, the expected relay distance is
\begin{equation}
  D_k^{\mathrm{ref,t}} = \frac{T}{n}(n_m v_m + n_f v_f),
  \label{eq:time_ref}
\end{equation}
The time-normalized score
\begin{equation}
  S_k^{(t)} = \frac{D_k}{D_k^{\mathrm{ref,t}}}
  \label{eq:score_time}
\end{equation}
is dimensionless and equals 1.0 for a team rowing exactly at the
composition-weighted reference pace.

\subsection{Power-based normalization}

We propose instead to compare power outputs directly.
The reference power for team $k$ is the composition-weighted mean of
individual reference powers:
\begin{equation}
  P_k^{\mathrm{ref}} = \frac{n_m P_m + n_f P_f}{n},
  \label{eq:pref}
\end{equation}
where $P_m$ and $P_f$ are the male and female reference powers
(from Table~\ref{tab:records}, rows 5--6).
The power-normalized score is
\begin{equation}
  S_k = \frac{P_k^{\mathrm{act}}}{P_k^{\mathrm{ref}}}.
  \label{eq:score}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Why power normalization is preferred}

The two methods treat the nonlinearity of $P \propto v^3$ differently.
Time normalization is linear in velocity: it credits a rower who rows
\SI{10}{\percent} above reference with a \SI{10}{\percent} bonus,
regardless of the absolute speed difference.
Power normalization is cubic: the same \SI{10}{\percent} velocity bonus
corresponds to $(1.10)^3 - 1 \approx \SI{33}{\percent}$ more power,
and this full advantage is reflected in the score.

More importantly, the Concept2 ergometer \emph{measures} power -- it is the
physically native quantity of the instrument.
Averaging power across team members, Eq.~\eqref{eq:pref}, correctly
weights each rower's physical contribution.
By contrast, averaging reference velocities and then converting to a
reference distance, Eq.~\eqref{eq:time_ref}, applies a linear average
where the true relationship is cubic, underestimating the advantage of
having high-power rowers on the team.

Power-based normalization also has a natural interpretation:
a score of $S_k = 1.0$ means the team delivered exactly the power
expected for their composition; $S_k > 1$ means they exceeded the
reference, and $S_k < 1$ that they fell short.

Figure~\ref{fig:norm_comparison} illustrates how the reference performance
depends on team composition and the choice of reference distance.
Panels~(a) and~(b) show the composition-weighted reference time and power
for all six possible compositions of a five-person team (5M/0W through
0M/5W) across the four available record distances (500\,m, 1000\,m,
2000\,m, 5000\,m).
Reference times increase monotonically with distance (panel~a) and are
nearly linear in the fraction of female rowers at each distance.
Reference powers decrease monotonically with distance (panel~b), and the
gap between the most male-heavy (5M/0W) and most female-heavy (0M/5W)
compositions narrows as the distance grows: the male/female power ratio
falls from 1.77 at 500\,m to 1.44 at 5000\,m, reflecting the greater
male advantage in short anaerobic efforts.

Importantly, the 2000\,m and 5000\,m reference powers lie very close to
each other (ratio 1.47 vs.~1.44, a $\approx$2\% difference), whereas the
step from 1000\,m to 2000\,m is $\approx$16\%.
This insensitivity to the 2000\,m vs.\ 5000\,m choice is visible across
all six compositions in panel~(b), and makes the 2000\,m selection robust.

Panel~(c) shows both reference time and reference power at the chosen
2000\,m distance as a function of team composition.
Reference time varies nearly linearly from 5:34.7 (5M/0W) to 6:21.1
(0M/5W), because time averaging is linear in the fraction of female
rowers.
Reference power, by contrast, decreases more steeply from 597\,W (5M/0W)
down to a minimum of 370\,W for a 1M/4W team, then rises slightly to
405\,W for the all-female 0M/5W team -- a direct consequence of the cubic
relation between velocity and power: the all-female world record holder
rows faster than the linear interpolation of mixed-team reference paces
would predict, and this faster pace translates into a disproportionately
higher reference power.
This non-linearity is exactly what the power-based normalization captures,
and what a time-based approach would miss.

\subsection{Discussion}

All scores are below 1.0, as expected when using world records as the
reference: no relay team rows at the pace of the current world-record
holder.
The scores therefore represent the fraction of world-record power
output delivered per rower for the given team composition.

Across all ten teams, the mean total distance was
\SI{8.60 \pm 0.76}{\km} (mean $\pm$ sample std.\ dev., coefficient of
variation 8.8\%).
The mean normalized score was 0.640, meaning that teams collectively
produced approximately 64\% of the world-record power expected for their
composition.
Despite the comparatively small spread in raw distances, the score range
is much wider (0.336--0.794), because the cubic power law amplifies pace
differences into larger performance gaps: a team covering 550\,m less
than average (one standard deviation) corresponds to a pace deficit of
only $\approx$3\%, yet a power shortfall of $\approx$9\%.

The power-normalized ranking differs substantially from a raw-distance
ranking for several teams.
CTH-GU covered the greatest total distance (9.511\,km) and also ranks
first, combining high output with a three-man composition
($P_{\mathrm{ref}} = 520$\,W).
Chalmers Roddklubb -- Team~1 ranks second with a two-man composition
($P_{\mathrm{ref}} = 482$\,W) and a slightly lower absolute distance.
G{\"o}teborgs Roddklubb -- the only all-female team -- ranks third,
having delivered 74.8\% of world-record power against the lowest
reference ($P_{\mathrm{ref}} = 405$\,W).

Chalmers Roddklubb -- Team~2 illustrates the penalty for a heavy-male
composition: despite covering 9.095\,km (fourth largest raw distance),
they rank seventh after normalization, because their four-man composition
sets a demanding reference bar of 559\,W.

The cubic power law amplifies pace differences into larger score
differences.
The rank-1 team (score 0.794) averaged approximately 1:34.6 per 500\,m
against a world-record reference of 1:27.7 per 500\,m -- a pace deficit
of only $\sim$7\%, yet a power shortfall of $\sim$21\%.

\section{Conclusion and Outlook}

We have presented a novel power-based normalization method for mixed-gender
indoor rowing relay competitions.
The fundamental argument is that rowing power scales as the cube of
velocity, Eq.~\eqref{eq:power}, so small velocity differences
between genders translate into large power differences.
Normalizing by power rather than by distance or time therefore provides
a physically motivated and instrument-native measure of relative team
performance.

Applied to the RRC~2026, the normalized ranking differs substantially
from the raw-distance ranking for several teams.
The correction is particularly significant for all-female and
predominantly male teams, illustrating the importance of accounting
for gender composition in mixed relay events.

The normalization algorithm is released as the open-source Python package
\texttt{ergrace}~\cite{ergrace} and can be applied to future editions
of the competition or similar mixed-relay formats.

Our proposed method can be further extended and applied to different
age and weight groups, including official handicap categories as defined
by World Rowing~\cite{WorldRowingClasses}.
Incorporating age-graded reference powers and lightweight classifications
would allow fair comparison across an even broader range of team
compositions, opening the approach to wider use in community and
masters rowing events.
